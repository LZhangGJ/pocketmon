First-time Kaggle competitor here — the competition is genuinely fun, thanks to the organizers for building it.

While iterating on my agent I ran into a practical problem: **I can't tell from the leaderboard whether a new version is actually better**, because the first ~15 high-K games seem to decide where a submission settles. So we measured it. All numbers below are from public episode data — you can pull the same for your own subs.

## Experiment 1: eight submissions, side by side

We uploaded 8 incremental versions of the same agent family — including **one byte-identical zip submitted twice** (#5 and #6):

| submission | first-15 record | low-info pairings (opp ≥250 below) | pts from those wins | score at window end |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| #1 | 9W-6L | 0 | — | 800 |
| #2 | 9W-6L | 0 | — | 800 |
| #3 | 10W-5L | 0 | — | 917 |
| #4 | 9W-6L | 0 | — | 906 |
| **#5 (same zip as #6)** | 9W-6L | 1 | +0 | **873** |
| **#6 (same zip as #5)** | 12W-3L | 1 | +5 | **923** |
| #7 | 11W-4L | 1 | +12 | 828 |
| #8 | 12W-3L | 2 | +23 | 925 |

Things that jumped out at us:
- **#5 vs #6: identical bytes, 50 points apart.**
- **#6 vs #7: records differ by one game, outcomes differ by 95 points** — *whom* you beat matters as much as *whether* you win, and you don't choose whom.
- In #8's window, two of the highest-K games were against opponents rated **284 and 386** (~400 below). Both wins together: **+23** — less than a third of one same-band loss (-71). #8 still finished 12W-3L → 925, but two of its fifteen high-K games were effectively spent for nothing.

## Experiment 2: after the window, scores freeze

For our 5 lanes that kept playing 20-70 more games after the window:

| lane | score at game 15 | score now (20-70 games later) | drift |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | 873 | 903 | +30 |
| B | 923 | 916 | -7 |
| C | 917 | 919 | +2 |
| D | 906 | 872 | -34 |
| E | 828 | 827 | -1 |

**Average absolute drift ≈ 15 points over dozens of games**, versus the 120+ point spread the window itself created. With ~5 pts/game after convergence (as others have measured) and a handful of episodes per hour, whatever the first 15 games say tends to stick for the rest of the competition.

## Why I'm posting

This makes iteration feel like a slot machine: I can't distinguish "my change helped" from "this copy rolled better opponents" without submitting the same thing several times — which burns slots and adds noise for everyone.

**Questions / gentle suggestions:**
- How do other teams evaluate version-over-version improvement? Offline self-play only? Multiple identical uploads?
- Would it be feasible to keep provisional pairings within a rating band (e.g. ±250), or add a few more provisional games at lower K? Either would make the early window more informative without changing who the strongest agents are.

Happy to share the scripts we used to pull these numbers — and curious whether your episode data shows the same pattern.

Knowing how the scoring works on the leaderboard, I do not want to track how strong the bot is based on it's placement in the leaderboard. I know that the practice of 'submit 2 bots at once' is good, to make sure that you have a better chance of having a stronger run with your first ~10 opponents.
This does feel similar to how other online ranked systems like League of Legends works. You need to more or less hope that you perform strongly in your first handful of games because those are very impactful at 'hardening' your initial ranking. Shifting ranks afterwards is challenging. I wonder if Chess is similar 🤔.
(**Disclaimer**: I have not had a successful bot score above 600, yet)

Locally, I am training each meta deck (decks that commonly appear in replay files) with it's own agent in a league against the other decks and my specific deck(s) that I want to submit. **For each archetype, I am taking the agent with the best overall win-rate against all other decks in my local self-play training sessions for now**. Since some decks are much more popular to face, like Alakazam and Crustle, I will probably include a weighting so that I choose to keep an agent that performs better into those decks, specifically (for example, I care much more about an agent that improved it's win rate into Alakazam by 5% than an agent that improved it's win rate into Abomasnow by 5%).
I haven't been happy about how my rewarding is set up, yet. So, I am not confident about whether I am choosing the 'best' improving agent. I am too worried that there are niche situations where my hard-coded reward will stifle learning so that it makes the optimal decision in a scenario that may come up once every ~1,000+ games (for example, choosing to discard or keep Boss's Orders on an educated guess of how many Switch / Night Stretcher's exist in my opponent's deck). 
In the meanwhile, I am tracking specific metrics for my decks such as:
- "How often am I playing <important card> and do I end up winning the game when I play it before turn X?" 
- "When I choose a 'tutor' card like Poke Pad, what cards exist in my board state and hand when I choose to use the card, and what card did I pick? Did I win this game?"
- "When I play against <Archetype(s)>, what attacks am I using into this matchup?"
- I plan to soon track "How often am I using/drawing cards against a mill deck, or a deck that appears to have more than 1 Xerosic's Machinations? What is my win rate based on how many of those cards I used, how many cards did I discard due to Xerosic's?"
This gives me confidence that my agent is doing the right things, even if my win rate does not appear to change.

Because I have not seen a bot score above it's starting 600 score, I clearly must be doing something wrong. ;) So, this advice may only be helpful in demonstrating "what not to do." Sorry if this does not make sense. I am very new to the machine learning space.
