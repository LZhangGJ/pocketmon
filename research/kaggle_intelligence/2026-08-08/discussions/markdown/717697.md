The concept of **sequencing **and **prize mapping** are important for almost every deck. 
Since I'm newer I'm not 100% confident I use the phrases 100% correct but I'm more correct than I'm not. ;-]
**Sequencing **is knowing the right actions to take to have the most information available. Here's a very common example - you have Fezandipiti Ex (Fez) and Drakloak and Dreepy in play, and you have a Poke Pad (Pad) in hand. Let's say your goal is to find Stage 1 Drakloak, Basic Dreepy, and Basic Duskull on your turn. The less optimal sequencing is to use Poke Pad first for one of your 3 targets, then using Fez's Flip The Scripts (Draw3). For example, maybe I poke pad for Drakloak so I guarantee have 2 Drakloak abilities to use in order to see more cards, but there's a chance I find more Drakloak in my Fez's Draw 3 afterwards. Instead, if we use the generic draw of Fez first, we have the opportunity to see any of our 3 finds - if we found an item Buddy-Buddy Poffin (Poffin) off of Fez draw, we now are guaranteed to find those 3 cards we want with Poffin and Poke Pad! At this point, it could be optimal to use the Poffin and Pad now: I need to ask myself, "With the cards I now have, if I use Drakloak's ability and see a Dreepy (replace Dreepy as many times as needed with other cards).. would I have wished I saw different options?" and then decide to 'thin' the deck of cards I didn't want to see with other cards in my hand, in order to improve my chances of seeing other cards. In this example I've described, I may prefer to use my Poffin and Pad before using my 2 Drakloak's abilities, because I perceive higher value in seeing other cards that I cannot specifically search for at this point (from Magic the Gathering, searching your deck for a specific card is called 'tutor' or 'tutoring' so you may see that in PTCG too). So, the basic lesson is **generic draw first, then search for specific cards**. The primary exception to this rule is if you guarantee know what you need to see in your deck, you will "**thin to win**" aka discard your deck in order to have a better chance to randomly draw a card you're seeking.
**Prize mapping** is basically asking, "How will I take knockouts in order to get 6 prizes this game?" People will say a series of numbers like 2-2-2 meaning "I will take 3 knockouts across 3 turns in a way that gets me two prizes (usually a 2-prize pokemon and not 2 single prize pokemon, but it depends) at a time." There's a lot of minutiae / things to focus in on around prize mapping.. usually matchup dependent. It ties in a lot with "**prize trading / prize racing**". Prize trading is simply knowing how many prizes you intend to give up as a result of your actions. A good trade can be taking a 2-prize knockout on Fez and losing a 1-prize Alakazam in return. This prize trading builds into and augments your prize race against your opponent. Your prize map may look like 2-2-2, and your opponent on the other end will be looking to disrupt your prize map. For example if you only need 2 prizes left to win and your opponent's board is single prizers plus one Meowth Ex, they could play Scoop Up Cyclone on Meowth to change into a single prize board state, now forcing you to play for one more turn.
Some decks strategize to 'make your opponent take 7 or 8 prizes' by forcing your opponent into a 1-2-2 prize map, and then benching a 3-prizer (a mega pokemon in this format) afterwards, ensuring your opponent has to work harder to reach their goal of passing 6 prizes. Legacy Energy is good to disrupt a prize map. Having cards like Boss's Orders let's you maintain your prize map in case your targets are not being pushed to the active. Some decks can maintain a 1 prize board state for a long time Grimmsnarl Froslass is good at this) until they want to evolve into their 2 prize pokemon.

On your 3rd point, I can concede that there are board states that are impossible to come back from. For 'beatstick' decks that rely on winning by aggressively taking prizes every turn, it can be clear that games are lost when the beatstick deck is at 6 prizes when their opponent is on 2 or 3, most games, especially if they don't have a way to 'buy more turns' to get back into the game. There are also some archetype matchups that are almost impossible to win regardless of your deck. 
Here's a couple relevant examples: Day 1 there were Abomasnow Ex decks, those almost auto-lose to a Crustle deck (Ceruledge Solrock decks also auto-lose to Crustle if they can't 'donk' them turn 1, but those decks aren't in the Kaggle meta). In the same way, Crustle auto-loses to Ethan's Typhlosion decks. So, some players put Ethan's Typhlosion into their Crustle decks (this is hilarious and would never happen IRL I think) just to auto-win and climb the ladder that was all Crustle decks, and then use Crustle to beat the opponents that Crustle auto-wins. Then Starmie decks started to become good (Starmie decks aren't very viable if you want to win a large tournament, but it does well into both Ethan's Typhlosion and Crustle decks). So for those archetypes, your strategy is usually "I hope I don't hit this opponent" instead of trying to make a game plan. Good players will still play optimally to cheat/scam a win, but against an equally skilled opponent, it's almost impossible.

Here's an explanation of the Alakazam vs Crustle matchup:
A standard Crustle Kangaskahn deck against an Alakazam Dudunsparce deck is a race for Crustle to get 1 more Mist Energy onto a pokemon than the Alakazam player has Enhanced Hammers - it's almost impossible for the Alakazam player to win once they are out of Enhanced Hammers and the opponent has a Mist in play. If the Crustle player's attacking pokemon is Crustle (that does 120 dmg), you have a win-con to cycle your active Dudunsparce - you continuously put a Dudunsparce into the active position, it takes 120 damage from Crustle, you then Runaway Draw the Dudunsparce, push a new Dunsparce to the active, evolve it to Dudunsparce, and set up a new benched Dunsparce to repeat the cycle. 
The Crustle player's counter to this is preserving their Boss's Orders to reach and KO the benched Dunsparce before it evolves to Dudunsparce, or attack the Mist and other energy to Kangaskhan which does enough damage to one hit knockout (OHKO) the Dudunsparce. The Kaggle agents may not know to do this.

The Indianapolis deck that won has Dedenne with the attack Electromagnetic Sonar (put a trainer card into your hand). This completely flips the Crustle matchup. The Crustle player can only attach one energy per turn, and they play almost exclusively special energy. So, if the Alakazam player can get Dedenne into the active and cycle/recursion their Enhanced Hammers before the Crustle player gets 3 energy attached to a pokemon, the Zam player is basically in a checkmate position to keep the Crustle player energy-less. Learning to use Dedenne I think would be very challenging - it has exponentially more branching decisions to make than an Alakazam deck without Dedenne (this is what most Kaggles decks are doing). There ***are*** some ways a Crustle Kangaskhan player can win into Alakazam Dudunsparce w/Dedenne, but it's very very hard to do and requires certain cards in your deck that I'm not seeing in the Kaggle decks (multiple Eri, basic energy, sometimes Team Rocket's Articuno, hand trimmer + multiple Xerosic's are good tech cards to beat Alakazam w/Dedenne).

**Decks better suited for agents to pilot:** There is a concept of a 'skill floor' and 'skill ceiling' to describe the limit of expression for a deck and it's mechanics. Low skill floors are easier to start playing, high skill floors require a lot of time investment or understanding to begin playing. Low skill ceiling means there is not much skill expression for a good player to demonstrate, high skill ceiling means there is a lot of room for expression for a good player to take advantage of. You can tell if a deck is good for an agent to learn faster by reading the pokemon in an average decklist and seeing if there are many decisions that have to be made by reading their abilities and attacks. For example, you can read Drakloak and Dragapult Ex from the Pult deck, and that hopefully is enough to explain that this deck has many more decisions to make from those 2 cards alone than most of the other decks, let alone the other cards used like Meowth ex.
Seems like decks that can get away with not having cards that make them make decisions are good. I saw a Brambleghast Comfey deck (Team was ~"boss's orders is all you need") that is in the top 30 with a rule-based deck, they have many cards that are 4-of copies, and I saw that they don't even have Boss's Orders which I think could improve their win rate a lot, but I think they're not including it because there's a lot of decisions to make around if and when to use that supporter, what pokemon to target, and comparing that vs. the value of using another supporter or not using a supporter. Even so, they're top 50 with a simple mill approach.
Alakazam has a low skill floor, but it is higher than Crustle. An Elgyem and Dedenne in your list make the skill ceiling for the deck incredibly high. Without Elgyem and Dedenne, I think the skill ceiling is at an average location.
Crustle has a very low skill floor - show your opponent Crustle, and you win most games. Abomasnow Ex has a very low skill ceiling - you're basically just gambling with Abomasnow's attack, your skill expression would be not decking out by choosing Abomasnow's other attack, passing, or using Kyogre to cycle your discarded energy back into your deck. 
Zoroark Ex, Dragapult Ex (all variants), and current control decks (they're not that good, but a good control player would be first place in this Kaggle ladder) have an incredibly high skill ceiling, and I would say their skill floor is higher than average. These would be hard decks for an agent to get good with.
"Big Basic" decks like Teal Mask Ogerpon decks, Raging Bolt decks, are easy for an agent to pick up. However, they need to be very good at beating Crustle decks in the current meta. So, I wouldn't recommend those decks unless it's built to beat Crustle (i.e. 2 baby Raging Bolt, 4 Crispin in a Raging Bolt list, or having something like Unfair Stamp + Passimian in a big basic list).
