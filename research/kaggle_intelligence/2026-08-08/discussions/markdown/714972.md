Thanks for your post!

In the early stages of the competition, I found that aggressive rush decks like Starmie ex and Lucario ex climbed the rankings quickly because their strategy is relatively simple — just pile up enough resources on the field and attack mindlessly. Since the competition was still in its infancy, this approach was very easy and effective for gaining points. However, as we moved into the mid-to-late stages, people gradually became more familiar with the competition and the decks, and such rush decks became easier to counter. Especially when those skilled at coding began to build slower, control-oriented decks, aggressive rush strategies often lost their edge — not to mention the later implementation of reinforcement learning, since control decks' pacing and decision-making align well with RL reward mechanisms. Of course, in any card game, even aggressive rush decks incorporate some element of tempo and pace management; it's not that aggressive rush is inherently doomed.

I'm currently experimenting with my own control strategy. I've lost many games. While some of players fork open-source decks, I've been learning from a wide variety of deck archetypes at the back of the pack. My heuristic code has already exceeded 3,000 lines to handle different matchups, and Codex has helped me a lot. Writing heuristics for control decks is extremely difficult — especially when reviewing replays, I often find myself helpless against my agent's stupid plays. But after continuous iteration, my win rate has surpassed 50% after three days of learning.

Tomorrow, I plan to put together a second deck — an aggressive rush strategy tailored to the current Kaggle leaderboard meta — to try climbing the ranks quickly. I believe many other participants will do the same.
