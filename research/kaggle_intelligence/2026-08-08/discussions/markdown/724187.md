# PokéForge Technical Postmortem

What we tried, what moved, what failed, and what I would not spend games on again.

For repository navigation and reproducibility notes, see the [README](../README.md). The [full project ledger](PROJECT_ledger.md) contains one summary for each of the 90 source reports. The two longer narratives are [PROJECT_writeup.md](PROJECT_writeup.md) and [PROJECT_writeup_v2.md](PROJECT_writeup_v2.md).

## Scope and boundary

PokéForge was built for a Kaggle Pokémon TCG ladder. Our best live result came from copying a strong public policy into our own runtime. Our original methods improved weak agents, sometimes by a lot, but did not match that mature policy under the campaign's limits.

Those limits matter:

| Constraint | Effect |
|---|---|
| Five live submissions per day | Packaging mistakes and weak fires consumed real budget |
| Scores needed about 24 hours to settle | Early ladder spikes were not trustworthy |
| Offline opponents were incomplete | Offline results could screen candidates, but could not settle them |
| Current top-meta policies were mostly unavailable as code | We could observe key matchups but could not train against the policies that defined the ceiling |
| The game was stochastic | Small gains needed large samples or paired evaluation |
| Kaggle's runtime differed from local execution | Local success did not imply a valid submission |

The fair conclusion is not that copying always beats invention. A mature public policy beat the policies we built from scratch within this budget. The detailed pass history is in the [ledger](PROJECT_ledger.md).

## 1. Baseline, deck repair, and turn planning

The first agent was a weighted option scorer. It assigned values to attack, pass, search, draw, evolution, energy attachment, and other legal actions.

It beat the simulator's random agent 66.0% over 50 games. Against real public agents it won 1 of 160 games, with a Wilson-95 lower bound of 0.001. The stub evaluation also exposed a seat effect: 15.0% from the first seat and 6.2% from the second.

Loss analysis found that 55% of the deck was basic energy. Cutting energy from 33 cards to 13 and adding search raised win rate from 0.6% to 9.2%, 22 of 240, without changing the policy. Four weight adjustments later confirmed at 13.2% adjusted WR over 520 games.

The first architecture change was turn-level planning. Coordinating attack and energy as one plan raised adjusted WR to 20.0%, Wilson lower 0.160. A combat probe then matched the engine's damage result in all 16 legal-attack cases tested. The remaining Dragapult gap was not damage arithmetic. It was a multi-turn prize race.

Details: [real baseline](../pokeforge/reports/pass03_benchmarks.md), [deck repair](../pokeforge/reports/pass04_benchmarks.md), [tuning](../pokeforge/reports/pass06_benchmarks.md), [turn planner](pass07_results.md), [damage probe](../pokeforge/reports/pass08_results.md).

## 2. Local rules, imitation, and shallow search

We tried to recover a strong Lucario pilot's edge by copying parts of its behavior.

| Method | Result | Decision |
|---|---:|---|
| One energy-acceleration rule | 25.0% vs 32.5% diagnostic baseline | Local rule hurt |
| Ten-line additive playbook | 35.0% vs 26.2%, but under the +10pp gate | Some signal, no promotion |
| High-authority overrides | 26.2% to 21.2% to 20.0% | More forcing made the policy worse |
| Free-standing imitation | 33.8%, 33.8%, 36.2%, 32.5% over four rounds | Stalled below parity |
| One-ply engine search | 30.0% mirror WR | Worse than the best imitation round |

The free-standing pilot disagreed with the expert on less than 8% of logged decisions. That did not translate into on-policy strength. Small errors sent the policy into states the teacher rarely reached. Local overrides also failed for a concrete reason: fixing one move could damage the rest of the turn, such as preventing a bad retreat by ending development.

Details: [diagnosis](pass10_results.md), [additive playbook](pass11_results.md), [overrides](pass12_results.md), [imitation](pass13_results.md), [one-ply search](pass14_results.md).

## 3. Reinforcement learning

A small value network trained from self-play produced the clearest original machine-learning gain. The first loop oscillated. Adding an all-history replay buffer and a slow target network produced mirror results of 42.0%, 39.5%, 45.5%, 40.5%, 49.0%, and 54.5%. The final run's Wilson lower bound was 47.6%.

The gain did not generalize:

| Experiment | Result |
|---|---:|
| Mirror-trained value net on three non-mirror opponents | Wilson lower 0.1762 |
| Four-deck league training | Wilson lower 0.1575 |
| Card-aware reactive policy | Wilson lower 0.1653, adjusted WR 0.195 |
| Teacherless A2C | Wilson lower 0.1777, adjusted WR 0.2083 |
| A2C with replay and target critic | Wilson lower 0.1793, adjusted WR 0.210 |

Deck-policy entanglement was severe. The Iono reference pilot was competent, while the card-net on the same deck fell to a Wilson lower bound of 0.0285.

DAgger raised single-select agreement from 0.61 to 0.74, while non-mirror WR stayed at 0.167. Three full-decision training regimes landed near the same range: 0.183, 0.217, and 0.208. A stateful hand-coded heuristic scored 0.743, Wilson lower 0.707, over 600 games on the comparison task.

We stopped investing in stateless per-option scoring nets under this representation and training setup. That was a budget decision, not a general claim about neural policies.

Details: [stabilized self-play](pass16_results.md), [generalization](pass17_results.md), [league training](pass18_results.md), [card-aware policy](pass19_results.md), [teacherless A2C](pass20_results.md), [deck entanglement](pass22_results.md), [DAgger](../pokeforge/reports/pass23_results.md), [full-decision controller](pass24_results.md), [stateful heuristic](pass25_stepA.md).

## 4. Generalists, specialists, and deck variants

The generalist controller had a clear tempo problem. Against 9,097 top-agent decisions, agreement was 84.7% on playing cards from hand, but 21.2% on energy attachment, 19.4% on evolution, and 27.5% on attack. Re-prioritizing attacks, abilities, evolutions, and energy raised pooled WR from 0.305 to 0.380. Card-aware targeting then improved off-policy agreement but reduced on-policy WR from 0.380 to 0.358.

Deck-specific policies did much better:

| Pilot | Result |
|---|---:|
| Dragapult specialist | 0.645 field WR, Wilson lower 0.588 |
| Our deck-agnostic policies on the same task | 0.138 |
| Lucario specialist | 0.500 field WR vs 0.165 incumbent |
| Water specialist | 0.490 mirror, 0.475 field |
| Durant mill specialist | 0.525 mirror vs 0.145 generalist |
| Durant mill field result | 0.110 |

The mill specialist cut self-deck-out from 64% to 33% and doubled deck pressure, but the deck remained weak against the field. A Lucario localizer found zero disagreements against its source across 4,954 decisions, so the residual mirror loss was not a missing single-pick rule.

Opening-hand simulation was useful for diagnosis, not prediction. A 50,000-draw model found a water variant that cut mulligans from 0.456 to 0.259. Its mirror result fell from 0.525 to 0.345. Better setup did not imply better play.

Details: [tempo diagnosis](pass27.md), [tempo fix](pass28.md), [targeting null](pass29.md), [Dragapult specialist](pass33.md), [specialist bake-off](pass35.md), [mill analysis](p35_mill_policy.md), [water specialist](pass37par.md), [consistency model](p37par_deck_consistency.md), [variant test](pass38.md).

## 5. Measurement and packaging

### Common Random Numbers

We interposed on the native shuffle so two policies could play the same deals. Identical policies produced identical trajectories. Variance reduction was about 4.88 times for near-clones and 1.18 times for dissimilar policies. The benefit had to be measured per comparison.

### Perturbation control

Replay rescue initially looked useful. Forcing a teacher's choices into losing lines rescued games. A random-choice control rescued at the same rate.

| Arm | Rescue rate |
|---|---:|
| Random first-K legal choices | 0.368 |
| Oracle bounded arm | 0.316 |
| LLM bounded arm | 0.316 |

The old comparison used an unchanged replay as the floor, which was near zero by construction. After the control, rescue claims had to beat a perturbation-matched floor.

### Packaging

Kaggle executed the agent in a bare namespace where `__file__` was undefined. Agents that used it at module load crashed before playing. Local tests also hid SDK dependencies.

The fix was a pure-Python SDK shim, a bundled card database, legal fail-closed behavior, and an isolated smoke test that extracted the tarball, denied the ambient SDK, loaded it in a bare namespace, and forced decisions. The next canary and competitive submissions both returned COMPLETE.

Details: [CRN](pass57_crn_feasibility.md), [perturbation control](pass65_positive_control.md), [packaging](pass31.md), [`__file__` failure](../pokeforge/reports/pass32.md), [first valid live fire](pass44.md).

## 6. Offline proxies and the live ladder

A live rating was an Elo fixed point, not a raw win rate:

```text
settled ≈ mean_opponent_rating + 400 * log10(p / (1 - p))
```

The expression is approximate because the opponent distribution changes as the agent climbs. It explained why an agent could have a losing record and still settle highest after reaching a stronger rating band.

The ladder also had operational rules: new agents seeded near 600, provisional ratings could overshoot, only the two most recent submissions played ranked games, and a result needed about 24 hours to settle.

The offline weighted-field gate failed as a final judge. A Dragapult candidate cleared at +0.059 with CI [+0.014, +0.104] against Alakazam, but the live ordering was Alakazam about 820.5 versus Dragapult about 797.8 at the recorded comparison. The offline pool contained too many of our own pilots.

A later Dudunsparce rebuild passed the offline gates and settled at 369.3. It added acceleration intended to reduce time-to-powered-attacker from turn 19 to about turn 5. The live result showed that setup speed was not the binding issue.

Key live results:

| Agent | Settled rating |
|---|---:|
| Alakazam copy | About 802 to 803 |
| Dragapult | About 780.5 |
| Lucario | About 754.5 |
| Dudunsparce rebuild | 369.3 |

The final Alakazam result was about top 27% of roughly 3,550 teams.

Details: [offline gate failure](pass61_field_gate.md), [Elo analysis](PASS68_ladder_intel.md), [Dudunsparce diagnosis](PASS69_inflight_diagnosis.md), [rebuild](PASS71_dudunsparce_rebuild.md), [Alakazam loss analysis](PASS72_alakazam_refire_and_analyze.md), [final monitor](PASS73_monitor_check.md).

## 7. Copying a mature public policy

The strongest agents were faithful copies of public policies, rewritten for the local runtime.

| Policy | Verification | Result |
|---|---:|---|
| Dragapult v2 | 416 of 416 sampled decisions matched | Mirror 0.500 vs 0.425 baseline; field lift not significant |
| Alakazam | 691 of 691 sampled decisions matched | Best live agent |
| Current Dudunsparce, Hop's Trevenant, and Starmie policies | No runnable policy found in the sourcing pass | Could not train against or copy them |

The decision matches are trace checks, not proof over the full state space. They showed that the rewrites followed the source on the sampled states.

From-scratch attempts did not match Alakazam under this budget:

| Method | Result |

## 8. Attempts to improve the copied policy

Local changes to strong policies did not produce a verified lift.

| Attempt | Result |
|---|---:|
| Constant tuning | 42 CRN pairs over six candidates; no 95% CI cleared zero |
| Immediate active-KO finish mode | Delta exactly zero over 73 paired games |
| Finish mode activation | About 23.8% of games, but only reordered actions |
| Shared execution layer | Changed the top choice on 0.83% of decisions |
| High-field loss patching | Misplays existed, but were scattered outside the ceiling matchups |
| Alakazam re-fire | Returned to about 803.6 |

Fresh high-field analysis rejected slow setup. Wins and losses both assembled by turn 4 and powered by turn 5. Wins ended around turn 11. Losses against the grinding decks extended to turns 17 to 19, where Alakazam's one-energy damage clock was outpaced.

We did not run full RL fine-tuning from the Alakazam copy. That remains unmeasured. It was also not a clean missing step. RL needed a cheap reward aligned with ladder strength and runnable versions of the opponents that defined the ceiling. We had neither. Prize, setup, and board-state shaping would reuse proxies that had already failed, while the clean ladder reward was too slow. Self-play had already produced 54.5% in the mirror and weak field transfer.

Details: [constant tuning](pass58_crn_ascent.md), [finish mode](pass59_finish_mode.md), [loss analysis](PASS72_alakazam_refire_and_analyze.md).

## 9. Signals I would not trust again

| Signal | What invalidated or limited it |
|---|---|
| Win rate against random | 66.0% became 0.6% against real agents |
| A locally correct rule | Energy acceleration rule lowered the diagnostic result |
| Imitation accuracy | Less than 8% disagreement still missed on-policy parity |
| Mirror RL | 54.5% mirror did not transfer to the field |
| More opponent variety by itself | Four-deck league training did not improve the field result |
| Better opening consistency | Lower-mulligan water variant lost the mirror |
| Replay rescue without a random intervention arm | Random choices rescued as often as teacher choices |
| An offline weighted field as the final judge | It ranked Dragapult over the stronger live Alakazam agent |
| Faster setup as a sufficient fix | Dudunsparce acceleration settled at 369.3 |
| Local runtime success | Kaggle's bare loader broke `__file__` and hidden SDK dependencies |
| Strategy prose as a usable teacher | The ceiling-defining policies were not executable |

## 10. Recommendations for other participants

1. Use random only for smoke. Benchmark against real public agents early.
2. Seat-swap evaluations and use lower bounds for promotion.
3. Separate mirror and non-mirror gates. Test transfer before investing further.
4. Fix deck structure before adding policy complexity.
5. Add turn coherence before writing many local rules.
6. Treat imitation agreement as a diagnostic, not an outcome metric.
7. Use CRN for near-clone comparisons, and measure its variance reduction.
8. Add a random perturbation arm to replay-rescue tests.
9. Treat offline opponent pools as screens. Let settled live results override them.
10. Reproduce the submission runtime in isolation and fail closed with legal actions.
11. Try specialists when a generalist is weak, but do not assume a specialist can rescue weak deck content.
12. Copying a mature public policy can be the rational use of a limited budget. Call it copying and test whether your changes improve it.

## Final read

Original methods raised a 0.6% agent into useful local regimes. Deck repair, turn planning, self-play, specialist control, CRN, perturbation controls, and packaging gates all worked for their intended purpose. They did not produce our best live policy.

The strongest sourceable policy was the Alakazam copy, which settled around 802 to 803. Local tuning and search patches did not improve it. The top opponents that defined its ceiling were not available as runnable policies, which also limited training and offline evaluation.

The durable result is the failure map. Random win rate, local rules, imitation agreement, mirror performance, setup consistency, replay rescue, and offline weighted fields all looked stronger than they were. The linked reports above contain the full protocols, sample sizes, and pass-level evidence.
