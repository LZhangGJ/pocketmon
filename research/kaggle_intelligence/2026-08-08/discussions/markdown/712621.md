I also think that the current rating system used in Kaggle Simulation competitions is not well suited for games with high randomness and strong matchup dynamics, such as the Pokémon Trading Card Game. In my view, the current leaderboard has several serious issues.

This was not a major problem in previous Simulation competitions, such as the recently finished Orbit Wars. In Orbit Wars, differences in skill translated into clear win-rate gaps, and matchup dependency between models was relatively small. However, I do not think the same system can be directly applied to the Pokémon Card Game.

I see four main problems.

**1. Too few games and too little rating movement after convergence**

Once an agent’s rating has more or less converged, the number of games it plays drops sharply, and the rating change per game becomes very small, often around 5 points.
For a highly stochastic game like Pokémon Card Game, where improving the win rate from 50% to 60% is already very meaningful, this number of games and this amount of rating movement are simply not enough.

**2. Excessively large rating swings immediately after submission**

Right after an agent is submitted, its rating volatility is extremely high. This makes the first problem even worse.

As a result, instead of “submit an agent and wait several days until its rating properly increases,” the optimal strategy becomes something like: “submit repeatedly, and if one submission happens to win several early games and reaches a high rating, stop submitting.”
This turns the submission process into something much more luck-based than it should be.

**3. Large variance in converged ratings due to randomness and deck matchups**

Because the game has both high randomness and strong deck matchup effects, I think it is entirely possible for the four publicly provided sample decks to beat even top-level decks in some games.

For example, a player can brick, place only one Basic Pokémon on the first turn, and immediately lose on the opponent’s following turn. Situations like this are perfectly possible in Pokémon Card Game.

Therefore, even against low-rated agents, it is not easy to win consistently. Combined with problem 2, this further encourages a gambling-like strategy where participants simply hope for an early winning streak right after submission.

**4. The rating range for opponent selection is too narrow**

Suppose someone builds a deck that is specifically strong against the current first-place deck. Even then, unless that deck first reaches a rating close to first place, it never be selected as an opponent for the top deck.

From the perspective of the first-place deck, its opponents are also concentrated among only a small number of high-rated decks. Once a deck reaches first place, it becomes much easier to optimize only against those few opponents.

Even if that deck is actually weak against many decks in the low-to-mid rating range, it can avoid facing them if it gets lucky early and skips past that rating range due to the issue described in point 2. In that case, it may never need to fix those weaknesses.

Given how much randomness and matchup dependency this game has, I feel that the current rating range used for opponent selection is too narrow.

Your point applies to the normal ladder-climbing logic of card games. For example, in card games like Hearthstone, each rank tier has a clearly defined deck meta, because players can adjust their decks appropriately based on the current meta and competitive dynamics. However, the problem is that in this Kaggle Pokémon TCG competition, you are not allowed to change your deck in one agent. Combined with the immature Elo rating rules, this leads everyone to gravitate toward decks that can gain rating quickly. But once they reach a high ranking, unless their rank is threatened by stronger decks, they are unlikely to switch decks easily — because under the Elo rating system, as the number of matches increases over time, the penalty for losing becomes smaller and smaller.

Making this post partially as a warning to other competitors and partially as a plea for change: the current scoring system is incredibly inconsistent. 

I first noticed this when I was experimenting with a kangaskhan/crustle deck, similar to the ones that have been performing well in the recent standard meta. After making what in local testing were improvements, the agent's leaderboard score regressed. I was curious, so I submitted two identical agents ([one](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/pokemon-tcg-ai-battle/submissions?submissionId=53879890&episodeId=81018137#) and [two](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/pokemon-tcg-ai-battle/submissions?submissionId=53892632&episodeId=81059056#)). While they were active, one converged to a score of 940.7 and the other 790.8 (~150pt gap). After this, I did some work on a different archetype, festival lead. I would expect this deck to have less polarizing matchups than crustle, but I observed the opposite. Like crustle, I submitted two identical agents. [One](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/pokemon-tcg-ai-battle/submissions?submissionId=53957772&episodeId=81422227#) is currently scoring 1104.6 while [the other](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/pokemon-tcg-ai-battle/submissions?submissionId=53957319&episodeId=81455280#) is currently scoring 687.4 (>400pt gap). This is a huge gap for identical decks that are played the same. To be fair to the scoring algorithm these have only been active for ~10hrs (and I will likely leave them active for awhile), but it's still concerning.

For now here's my advice to others: if you want a good picture of how you stack up vs the leaderboard meta submit each agent twice. You can have two agents active at once, and as long as you're not running into the daily submissions cap you can a clearer picture this way. Beyond that, running local evaluations vs the example decks and other public agents is a great way to test and refine on a much larger (but less varied) sample size.

I'm not sure how this can be fixed. I would guess playing more games per agent and/or converging an agent's rating slower could help? It appears an agent's fate is largely decided by its first 5-10 games, although I don't have the full episode dataset so I can't get a clear picture. If nothing changes, I expect the top of the leaderboard will be submitting their final agents over and over until they "high roll" and get a good score. We [already see](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/pokemon-tcg-ai-battle/discussion/712476) people hesitant to switch off of high scoring leaderboard agents, which is bad for the competition imo.

Hopefully this can be fixed, and good luck to everyone!

I’m seeing very similar behavior on my side.

We submitted effectively the same agent multiple times. The best run reached 1100+ public score, while another run of the same agent is currently only in the 800s. Both have been active for about a day, so this does not look like a temporary “just submitted” rating artifact anymore. The ratings seem mostly converged, but the gap is still huge.

This also makes local validation very hard to interpret. In our local round-robin / public-agent pool, one RL/ranker-style agent was the strongest candidate, but on the Kaggle leaderboard it only converged to around 965. Meanwhile other variants that were not clearly better locally could reach much higher leaderboard scores. So the leaderboard result seems to depend heavily on the initial matchmaking path and matchup distribution, not only on actual agent strength.

I think this issue is especially severe for Pokemon TCG because the game has:
- high variance from draws and prize cards,
- very polarizing deck matchups,
- fixed decks per submission,
- and a relatively small number of games per agent.

As a result, the first few games can put an agent into a favorable or unfavorable rating/opponent region, and that early path may dominate the final score for a long time.

I agree with the suggestion that, under the current system, submitting the same final agent twice may give a more realistic estimate than a single submission. But ideally the scoring system would reduce this variance, for example by playing more games per submission, using a wider opponent range, showing uncertainty/confidence intervals, or evaluating duplicated submissions with shared/randomized matchmaking seeds.

Right now it feels difficult to distinguish “this agent is truly better” from “this copy got a better early matchmaking path.”

Every 100 matches I took a snapshot of the leaderboard and measured how much each agent's ranking changed. Here's the full results:
