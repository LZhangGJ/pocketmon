The same policy can gain or lose a surprising amount of ladder strength when the deck changes. Yet many participants still test against only one or two public baselines. I curated this dataset to make a diverse, machine-ready deck population available to everyone in the competition.

## What is included

- 22 exact, validated 60-card lists (EX_01-EX_22)
- strategic labels for tempo, prize profile, setup complexity, and pilot complexity
- damage engines, resource engines, key decisions, strengths, and risks
- card-usage statistics and a complete 22 x 22 similarity matrix
- one-line Card ID deck exports ready for the competition format
- a reproducible analysis Notebook with four reusable figures

## Three practical uses

### 1. Train on a population, not one favorite deck

Sample across aggressive, midrange, control, toolbox, and combo lists. A simple curriculum is to start with low-complexity tempo decks, then add spread damage, disruption, copied-attack toolboxes, and multi-engine lists.

### 2. Build a genuinely diverse evaluation panel

The similarity matrix measures quantity-aware card overlap. Pick low-similarity lists when you want broad coverage, and high-similarity pairs when you want a controlled test of how small deck changes affect the same policy.

### 3. Diagnose deck-policy interaction

Hold your policy fixed and rotate all 22 decks. Break results down by prize profile and complexity. This often reveals failures hidden by a single aggregate score: bench management, prize mapping, energy allocation, evolution timing, or long-horizon damage placement.

## A few structural observations

- Similar-looking prize plans can rely on very different resource engines.
- Single-Prize decks are useful stress tests for attack continuity and six-KO planning.
- Spread-damage and toolbox lists expose much larger decision spaces than direct tempo decks.
- Shared staples do not imply identical play: quantity-aware similarity helps separate common Trainer shells from the archetype's real engine.

## Limits

V1 is a deck-intelligence dataset, not a tier list. It contains no simulated matchup win rates and makes no claim that one deck is intrinsically strongest. EX_10 is structurally valid but marked medium source confidence because it was resolved from a named-list transcription; the other 21 lists come from canonical Card ID CSVs.
