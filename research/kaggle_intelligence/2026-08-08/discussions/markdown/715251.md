I would like to raise a concern about the current evaluation and matchmaking structure from the perspective of agent development.

I understand the need to keep parts of the evaluation hidden. Hidden opponents, hidden rating details, and live matches can all be useful for preventing overfitting or direct optimization against the leaderboard. I am not asking for the full rating formula or matchmaking system to be exposed.

However, the current feedback loop makes it very difficult to determine whether an agent update is actually an improvement.

In my experience, nearly identical versions of the same deck and agent, submitted around the same time, can end up with rating differences of around 100 points. This seems to depend heavily on when a submission enters the queue, the initial opponents it faces, and the limited number of matches it receives.

Because matches may be sparse for several hours, a participant cannot obtain enough games to distinguish a real improvement from normal variance. In Pokémon TCG, turn order, opening hands, deck matchups, prizes, and in-game randomness can all have a substantial effect on individual match results. When only a small number of live matches are available, leaderboard movement is not a reliable A/B testing signal.

This creates a difficult development problem:

A rating increase after an update may reflect a genuine improvement, favorable initial matchmaking, or favorable matchups in a small sample.

A rating decrease after an update may reflect a real regression, unfavorable queue timing, or unfavorable matchups in a small sample.

As a result, participants cannot reliably decide which agent version should become their final submission.

There is also a concern about the high-rating pool becoming too locally closed. If high-rated submissions mostly face only nearby submissions, the leaderboard may increasingly reward agents that counter the current small high-rating meta rather than agents that are robust across a broad opponent distribution.

This can make final-submission selection into a meta-prediction problem: participants may need to guess what other top teams will submit, rather than selecting the agent with the most generally robust performance.

I think a stronger evaluation loop would benefit both participants and the competition itself. Possible improvements could include:

1. A local or hosted simulation benchmark using the official starter decks and starter agents.
2. Fixed seeds and reproducible match manifests, so participants can compare two versions of an agent on exactly the same test set.
3. A provisional benchmark score based on standardized matches against fixed opponent agents.
4. Basic evaluation diagnostics, such as number of matches, opponent-distribution summary, expected win probability, or rating uncertainty. The full internal rating system does not need to be exposed.
5. Broader randomized matchmaking within a sufficiently large top-tier pool, rather than relying mostly on a narrow local rating neighborhood.
6. Optional standardized behavioral indicators, such as timeout rate, illegal-action rate, obvious missed lethal opportunities, or loop frequency.

Live matches should still matter because they test adaptation and robustness against unknown opponents. However, sparse and non-reproducible live rating should not be the only meaningful development signal available to participants.

A standardized benchmark would allow participants to detect regressions, validate improvements, and select final submissions based on reproducible evidence rather than queue timing and short-term leaderboard variance.

I believe this would make the competition more informative, fairer, and more valuable as an agent-development task.

### Message 3483398 — unknown

I agree that participants should build their own internal benchmarks, and I am doing that.

My concern is not that participants need more help developing agents. The concern is that an internal benchmark cannot reproduce or validate the official leaderboard signal when match volume, opponent distribution, matchmaking conditions, and rating uncertainty are all controlled by the host.

“Everyone is in the same position” does not make the resulting ranking a reliable measure of agent quality. If identical submissions can differ by more than 100 rating points depending on their evaluation path, then leaderboard movement is difficult to interpret as evidence of improvement or regression.

A participant-built benchmark is useful for local development, but it does not replace a reproducible official evaluation baseline. Both can coexist: participants can innovate on their own benchmarks, while the host provides a minimal standardized reference point for interpreting leaderboard results.
